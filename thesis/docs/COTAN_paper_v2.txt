Abstract
Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) now routinely profiles thousands of individual transcriptomes, yet no consensus pipeline exists for
either laboratory protocol or downstream analysis. Droplet-based datasets are especially challenging: their count matrices are extremely sparse
and many biologically important genes are expressed at very low levels. Standard workflows, borrowed from bulk RNA-seq, apply normalization,
log-transformation, and feature filtering, steps that can distort true-zero counts and systematically discard low-expression regulators. A method
that natively handles sparsity, avoids imputation, and retains informative low-abundance genes is therefore desirable. We present an extended
version of the COTAN workflow, a framework based on gene correlations that models zero counts directly, requires no log-normalization or scaling,
and is tailored to UMI droplet data. COTAN’s key features are (i) robust gene–gene correlation matrices, validated on diverse public datasets, (ii)
a superior detection of differentially expressed genes, including those with very low expression, consistently outperforming leading tools, (iii) a
highly sensitive statistical scoring, able to detect heterogeneous cell groups and to confirm whether a cluster is truly transcriptomically uniform.
Together, these advances provide a biologically grounded, end-to-end solution for scRNA-seq analysis. COTAN is available in Bioconductor and
on GitHub. Supplementary information is available at this site.

Introduction
Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) enables the simultaneous
profiling of the transcriptomes of thousands of individual cells
and has rapidly become a routine tool in molecular biology.
Although the technique was first described in 2009, there is
still no universally accepted gold-standard pipeline, either for
the wet-lab protocol or for downstream data analysis. The optimal analytical strategy often depends on the biological question being addressed. At present, many widely used platforms
are droplet-based [1–3], incorporate Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMI) [1–4] and pose substantial computational challenges [5–8], chiefly because of the extreme sparsity of the
count matrix [9, 10] and the frequent presence of key genes
expressed at very low levels [11, 12].
Most analytical pipelines [13–17], inspired by the bulk
RNA-seq approach, apply normalization, scaling, and logtransformation to render cell-to-cell expression comparable, often followed by feature selection that excludes lowly
expressed genes. While these steps can be useful, they
may also introduce biases such as artificial drop-outs [18]
and make it virtually impossible to study genes expressed
at low levels [11]. Unfortunately, many biologically crucial genes, including key regulators, fall into this category
[12].

To overcome these limitations, COTAN does not rely on
log-transform, normalization, or scaling steps. Our model
treats the observed counts as Poisson samples from an underlying parametric gene-expression distribution, in line with
similar models used in [18, 19–21] and corresponding to the
large number approximation of multinomial models like [22].
We fit one “library size” parameter per cell and two parameters per gene, with the critical choice of modeling the probability of zero counts of each gene, besides the average. So,
we do not assume an extra zero-inflation mixture component
beyond an overdispersed count model; instead, we calibrate
parameters using the observed zero frequency. COTAN does
not need imputation and is able to bypass the sparsity problem [10].
The new COTAN release preserves the original mathematical core [23] while providing a more rigorous evaluation
of gene–gene correlations and extending co-expression analysis [24–26] into an end-to-end scRNA-seq workflow. We
updated the pipeline by improving and extending the cleaning and plotting functions, adding a new gene-enrichment
function that is competitive with state-of-the-art tools (especially for lowly expressed genes), and implementing two clustering strategies. COTAN is now easier to run and, on average, around seven times faster on typical datasets than the

Materials and methods
Theoretical model
COTAN is based on the computation of a gene co-expression
matrix (coex) C between genes. Each matrix element Ci, j represents the co-expression coefficient between the two genes i
and j, a number typically in the range (−1, 1), similar to a correlation coefficient between the expression levels of the two
genes across cells. The coefficient Ci, j measures the deviation
between the 2 × 2 contingency table of the joint occurrence
of genes i and j in the cells, and the expected co-expression
assuming independence between i and j (see “Correlation matrix” in Supporting materials).
The formula for Ci, j is a modified version of the Phi coefficient of the contingency table, which also corresponds to
the Pearson correlation of the binarized counts (see “Phi coefficient” in Supporting materials). The main difference is the
method used to estimate the expected number of cells expressing both genes. In a standard contingency table this quantity
would be estimated proportionally to the marginals, but for
single-cell datasets this yields spurious correlations due to the
variable library size of cells. In fact, cells with lower detection
efficiency will generally have more zeros across genes, creating artificial co-dropouts and resulting in an overestimation
of the gene correlations. coex instead estimates the expected
number of cells expressing both genes using a robust probabilistic model with two parameters for each gene and one for
each cell. This model is described in detail in [23]. In brief,
for each gene g in each cell c, it estimates the probability of a
zero count using a negative binomial distribution with mean
λgνc and shape parameter αg. Here, λg represents the average
expression of gene g, νc is proportional to the library size of
cell c, and αg is calibrated from the observed number of zeros for gene g. A key feature of the model is that it is focused
on the zero occurrences, rather than to the entire read-count
distribution.

Datasets
To evaluate our tool’s performance, we selected a diverse collection of datasets (21 in total, coming from 13 different experiments, listed in Supplementary Table S1), spanning both
early and recent studies and covering a range of sequencing efficiencies and protocols, in some cases providing cellclustering information [1, 28–34].
Some datasets were formed by different batches, runs or
developmental time points. In these cases, the main dataset

was divided in more subsets. Most datasets have UMI but we
also tested three different datasets without them.
For the La Manno dataset [35], we selected the time points
E13.5, E15.0, and E17.5 and restricted further to neuronal
cells (classified as “neuron” by the top clustering level included
in the original study). For the Brown PBMC dataset [36] we
selected three runs with an intermediate cell number.
We also defined refined versions of the La Manno and
Brown datasets, both consisting of 15 clusters from the original analysis that were chosen for their large size and internal
homogeneity (see Supplementary Table S3 and the “Use of
datasets” section in Supporting materials, for details).

COEX matrix
Tools such as Seurat [15], Scanpy [17], and Monocle [14]
compute the normalized expressions of genes, from which one
can also obtain a gene correlation matrix. Other frameworks
like CS-CORE [37] and Scenic [38], are specifically intended
to study gene co-expression, without log-transformation or
scaling and without filtering lowly expressed genes. COTAN
falls in the same group, and the coex should be interpreted
as an alternative co-expression or correlation matrix between
genes.
We compared coex with the gene correlations from the
other tools, by assessing the distribution of the associated pvalues for pairs of genes that should and should not be independent. In fact, correlation coefficients Ci, j over m cells can
be converted to p-values pi, j for the null hypothesis of gene
independence, with pi, j = P(χ 2 (1) > m Ci,2 j ) (where χ 2 (1) is
a chi-squared random variable with one degree of freedom).
The analysis was performed separately on the 12 mouse brain
and 7 human PBMC datasets of Supplementary Table S1.
For the mouse brain datasets we selected 16 constitutive
and 45 neural marker genes (in particular, we selected some
neural progenitor genes, some pan neuronal genes, and some
cortex layer marker genes). Similarly, for human blood we
selected 20 constitutive transcription factors [39] and 38
PBMC cell markers (some CD4 T cell markers, B cell and
other markers specific for other PBMC subpopulations, see
Supplementary Table S2 for the complete list). Marker genes
should typically show significant correlation (either positive
or negative) with each other, since they are specific only to
some sub-populations (no independence, alternative hypothesis). On the other hand, when one or both genes are constitutive, since their expression is roughly uniform, the correlation
should be typically low (independence, null hypothesis).

gene i occurs or not. A correlation coefficient Di,A is then

GDI and UT clusters
Starting from the coex matrix, COTAN computes a gdi for
each gene. For gene i, the gdi is based on the lower tail of
the pairwise association p-values obtained by testing gene i
against all other genes j: we first compute p̄i , the mean of
the smallest 5% of these p-values, and then apply the transformation gdii = ln(− ln( p̄i )) which yields a more compact
and readily interpretable distribution of index values (see
also [23]).
This construction is motivated by the expectation that constitutively expressed genes exhibit uniformly weak associations with other genes, producing a lower-tail p-value distribution that is approximately uniform on [0,0.05]. By contrast,
genes whose expression varies across cell sub-populations
tend to show both positive and negative associations with sets
of cell-type enriched genes, leading to an excess of very small
p-values in the lower tail. Under the null hypothesis of expression independence from cell sub-population, the median gdi
is therefore expected to be ln(− ln(0.025)) ≈ 1.305.
The 5% cutoff was selected because, in typical datasets, it
yields on the order of 500–1000 genes, consistent with the
magnitude of cell-type “elevated” gene sets reported in atlas

studies [42]. Higher gdi values indicate genes that show significant association with many others and thus provide an empirical measure of within dataset differentiation. Notice that
the gdi values depend on the specificity of the dataset taken
into account. Generally, via empirical testing, gdii > 1.5 suggests that gene i may be differentially expressed and supports
follow-up with dea. On the other side, values < 1.4 can be
associated with relatively uniform expression.
This observation suggests that, if COTAN is applied to a
subset of cells, rather than to the entire dataset, it is possible to examine the upper tail of the genes’ gdi values to decide
whether that subset is homogeneous or contains multiple subpopulations. In practice, one might, for instance, see whether
the highest percentile of gdis exceeds a suitable threshold chosen accordingly to the above levels, and conclude whether the
subset contains multiple sub-populations. The specific value
of the threshold, however, should be adjusted to suit the specific dataset and the level of sensitivity needed.
To assess the sensitivity of this approach to small deviations from homogeneity, we performed a large number of experiments on samples with two types of cells, varying both
their proportion and the degree of difference between their
types (see Supplementary Fig. S1). We considered pairs of clusters from the refined La Manno dataset (see Supplementary
Table S3). Each possible pair of clusters was combined at several mixing ratios, and for every synthetic sample we recorded
the top 1% of the gene-wise gdi distribution. Finally, we analyzed the dependence of this quantity on the two covariates.
Whatever the exact criterion on the tail of gdi distribution
one chooses, if a subset of cells passes the homogeneity test,
we call it a Uniform Transcript (ut) cluster.
To evaluate whether the ut label reflects a biologically relevant property, we examined its association with our ability to
distinguish two cell types within a cluster. We created mixtures
by pairing clusters from the refined La Manno dataset, selecting some that were still classified as ut and others that were
not. For each mixture we trained a logistic-regression model to
predict the two underlying classes and recorded its accuracy.
To ensure that the trained models were biologically meaningful and not statistical artifacts, the input variables were the
normalized expression levels of just the top 100 highly variable genes.

Applications
Clustering with the COEX
The coex matrix also allows for the recovery of useful information for clustering cells.
In conventional scRNA-seq workflows, the gene-expression
matrix is first reduced with Principal Component Analysis
(PCA), and the resulting low-dimensional representation is
then clustered with a shared-nearest-neighbor modularityoptimization algorithm. Instead, we compute the eigenvalues
and eigenvectors of the coex matrix and project the data onto
the eigenvectors associated with the largest eigenvalues, using this projection as the dimensionality-reduced input for
clustering.
To make this strategy effective, we replace raw normalized
expression values with a statistic that couples the observed
pattern of zero counts with their modeled probabilities (see
the “Mathematical framework” section in [23]). Specifically,
we use the p-derivative of the log-likelihood for each binaz

To evaluate the false-positive rate, cells from a single cluster

However, a completely random split would yield very few false
positives. To work in more challenging conditions, we instead
partitioned the cells based on library size. For each cluster, we
generated “high-” and “low-” library-size subsets across five
different proportion splits, and counted the number of genes
each method identified as significantly enriched (adjusted pvalue < .05).

which is derived from the collection of p-values across all
gene-cluster pairs. For this comparison we could also include
the differential expression tool of scVI [20, 41], which ranks

To evaluate our clustering algorithm, we applied it to a wellcharacterized scRNA-seq dataset of seven human lung-cancer
cell lines (see Supplementary Table S1, CL Arigoni), whose
true identities are known independently of any clustering procedure. We benchmarked our method against two Seurat
workflows: standard and SCTransform [15, 16, 43]. For all
of them, we varied the resolution parameter from 0.16 to 0.50
in steps of 0.02. This range yields 10–15 clusters, allowing all
methods to be compared with the same number of clusters
when calculating performance metrics. In fact, although there
are seven original cell lines, the UMAP embedding (as shown
in Supplementary Fig. S3) suggests at least 10 distinct transcriptional states, including three small “outlier” clusters.
The metrics we chose evaluate clustering quality from two
complementary sides: entropy-based homogeneity (EH) measures within-cluster purity, whereas entropy-based completeness (EC) measures how fully members of the same true class
are grouped together. Their chance-corrected versions, adjusted mutual information (AMI) and adjusted Rand index
(ARI), combine homogeneity and completeness into single,
overall agreement scores.
The CD14 dataset
To assess the ut clustering approach from a biological perspective, we clustered a nearly uniform dataset: the FACSsorted CD14+ (see Supplementary Table S1, CD14). Such a
dataset is useful because it can test a tool’s ability to detect

and identify even subtle differences among closely related cell
types. We compared COTAN with two automatic clustering
and labeling methods: CellTypist [44] and Azimuth [43].
For each COTAN cluster, significantly enriched genes were then
identified with the dea function (adjusted p-value < .05), used
to label the clusters, and submitted to gene-enrichment analysis using STRING [45, 46] to identify the cluster nature and
characteristics.

We compared coex with Pearson correlation computed on
normalized gene expression levels obtained by Seurat,
Scanpy and Monocle. We also included CS-CORE, the
Spearman correlation of the raw UMI counts (which Scenic

counts (binary).

Gene enrichment
We compared dea with analogous functions in Seurat,
Scanpy, Monocle, Memento, and scVI.
To evaluate false positives, we arbitrarily split each uniform
cluster by library size into two subsets, and applied the differential expression tools (see Fig. 2). While all methods identified numerous genes as significantly enriched, COTAN consistently exhibited the lowest false-positive rate, owing to its
robust probabilistic model that effectively accounts for library
size.
To evaluate sensitivity and specificity for identifying enriched or depleted genes, we applied each method to heterogeneous cell mixtures constructed from pure clusters and quantified performance using ROC curves (Fig. 3 and Supplementary
Figs S8 and S9). COTAN achieved the best overall performance,
sometimes on par with scVI, whereas the remaining methods showed consistently lower accuracy. Notably, scVI reports posterior probabilities rather than frequentist p-values,
which complicates calibration to a fixed significance threshold. COTAN was particularly sensitive for lowly expressed
genes (Fig. 3D), an important property given that many regulatory factors are expressed at low levels.

GDI sensitivity
We generated 1050 synthetic mixtures by blending two cell
types at different mixing ratios and, for each mixture, computed the 99th percentile of the gene-wise gdi distribution.
The resulting box plots (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S5)
show a clear, monotonic rise in this top gdi percentile as the

fraction of the second cell type increases. The steepness of
the rise is governed by the transcriptomic distance between
the two parent clusters (such distance is defined in “Use of
datasets” in Supporting materials). For very similar cluster
pairs (panel 1) the growth is slow, confirming that gdi is resilient to minor perturbations. By contrast, introducing even
5% of cells from a distant cluster (panel 6) markedly elevates the top gdi percentile. All cells in this experiment came
from the same dataset and were annotated as neurons, so even
the “distant” pairs represent comparatively subtle biological
differences. We also tested the same procedure on a setting
where cell transcriptomes are even more similar, restricting
the analysis to CD4 and CD8 T cells from Brown PBMC
Run 40. The results were comparable, but with lower values
(Supplementary Fig. S7).
We also repeated the experiment using outsider cells randomly drawn from two distinct clusters, to ensure the observed sensitivity was not an artifact of introducing cells from
a single external cluster (see Supplementary Fig. S6).
Overall, the results show that introducing even a small fraction of transcriptionally distinct cells leads to a pronounced
increase in the top gdi percentile.
We investigated whether a mixture’s ut label can predict
how accurately its constituent cell types can later be separated.
As illustrated in Fig. 5, which shows logistic regression accuracy versus ut status, mixtures classified as ut (or borderline
ut) consistently yield lower classification accuracy (datasets
on the left) than mixtures flagged as non-ut (datasets on the
right). In other words, when COTAN detects heterogeneity (the
ut criterion fails), the underlying cell types can be reliably distinguished, often using the expression of only a handful of
genes.

Discussion
In this study, we examined the main features of COTAN and
demonstrated its advantages for single-cell RNA-sequencing
analysis. The method embraces a co-expression focused strategy: instead of working with normalized expression levels,

it analyzes the simple presence or absence of transcripts (binarization of read counts). This directly tackles the problem
of data sparsity. Our benchmarks show clear performance
gains. Because the workflow removes the need for both normalization and imputation, it avoids the biases and artificial
dropouts that those steps can introduce, thereby preserving
genuine biological signals, especially for low-abundance yet
functionally important genes.
By summarizing single-cell data analysis with a reliable gene
correlation matrix, we enable the detection of subtle gene associations and maintain robustness against spurious correlations (Fig. 1).
Compared to established pipelines, COTAN provides a more
sensitive and accurate identification of cell population structure and their marker genes. It consistently outperformed
the other tools in our benchmarks for differential expression
(marker) detection, achieving higher true-positive rates and
markedly lower false-positive rates (Figs 2 and 3).
In clustering analyses, COTAN-derived clusters showed a
closer alignment with known biological classifications, reflected in superior clustering metrics (ARI, AMI, EC, EH)
compared to Seurat-based workflows (Fig. 6). For the ut
clustering, the test performed on a simple case study such
as the FACS-sorted CD14+ dataset, results in a biologically
meaningful dataset partitioning that splits the monocytes into
classical, intermediate and DC-derived monocytes. The ut
method lays the foundation for a new approach to clustering
focused on a biologically grounded cell partitioning.
These improvements are not merely numerical: they translate into clearer biological insights. For example, COTAN was
able to discriminate fine-grained cell subtypes (such as distinct

We must also acknowledge several limitations of COTAN
that temper these advancements.
First, COTAN lacks built-in mechanisms for batch effect
correction or dataset integration. This is a significant concern for multi-sample studies, where technical differences between runs or subjects must be accounted for. Because our
framework operates on raw counts and treats each dataset
as is, differences in sequencing depth or protocol could potentially confound the co-expression analysis. Some literature suggests that working with raw counts diminishes the
need for batch correction (arguing that major differences reflect biology rather than technical bias). We plan to investigate whether this holds true under a variety of conditions for
COTAN. Nonetheless, we recognize that a robust solution for
integrating across batches (perhaps via modeling latent factors or extending the contingency table approach to multiple datasets) will greatly enhance COTAN’s utility in large-scale
projects. Until then, users might need to apply external batch
correction heuristics or limit COTAN analyses to within-batch
comparisons.
Secondly, the interpretation of COTAN’s results requires an
understanding of its novel metrics. While we have provided visualization tools and documentation, there is a learning curve

in thinking about gene–gene co-expression significance and
the gdi, as opposed to more familiar concepts like fold-change
or cluster-average expression. We encourage users to use the
plotting functions (e.g. co-expression heatmaps, gdi distributions) to build intuition about these outputs.
Lastly, because COTAN operates on binarized read counts,
it can be less sensitive to changes in expression magnitude
among highly expressed genes. In particular, when a gene is
detected in all or nearly all cells, the model may not flag
it as differentially expressed even if sub-populations exhibit
large log-fold changes. In ongoing development of the probabilistic framework, we will evaluate strategies to mitigate this
limitation.
A future avenue is extending COTAN’s concepts to multimodal data (for example, integrating single-cell ATAC-seq coaccessibility with RNA-seq co-expression, to identify regulatory networks)—though this is speculative, the contingency
table framework could, in theory, be generalized to binary
events beyond gene expression presence/absence.
Finally, continued benchmarking on diverse datasets (including those with known ground truths or well-annotated
cell types) will be important. The fact that COTAN tends
to better adjust to sparsity and low-count genes is an advantage that could result in better ability to detect rare
cell types or to handle heterogeneous tissues (we have already seen a better resolution for detecting fine-grained cell

While COTAN showed clear advantages in our tests, every method has scenarios where it excels or falters. Ongoing evaluations will help identify such scenarios–for instance,
how does COTAN perform on extremely large cell atlases, or
on datasets with very low sequencing depth? Gathering this
knowledge will guide further optimizations and inform users
about when COTAN is the method of choice.
In conclusion, COTAN represents a significant advancement
in single-cell transcriptomics analysis, offering an innovative
approach that complements, and in many aspects enhances,
existing methods. Its focus on gene co-expression and careful
statistical treatment of sparse data allows it to uncover biological patterns that might otherwise remain hidden, thereby
enriching the interpretation of cellular heterogeneity. We have
shown that it achieves state-of-the-art performance in gene
correlation accuracy, differential gene expression detection
and cell clustering, underscoring its potential to become a
valuable component of single-cell analysis workflows. By addressing its current limitations and expanding its toolkit, we
anticipate that future iterations of COTAN will further empower researchers to dissect complex single-cell datasets, ultimately leading to deeper insights into gene regulation and cell
identity at the single-cell level.
