\section{Preprocessing Pipeline}

This section follows a single \gls{scRnaSeq} experiment from its data-generating mechanism to the \gls{countMatrix} consumed by every later analysis. Its scope is the route this thesis actually takes---droplet chemistry, pseudo-alignment, expectation-maximization---with the alignment alternative noted only where it changes the outcome. Plate-based and alignment pipelines differ in the details of each phase, not in the phase structure. It treats that path as a four-phase pipeline: ingestion and quality control, mapping, probabilistic resolution of ambiguous reads, and deduplication into a matrix. Each phase is mathematically and biologically constrained by the one before it, so the physical hardware chosen in the initial step dictates the shape, \gls{sparsity} and content of the final analytical matrix.

\subsection{Phase 0: Choosing the Protocol}

The design of a \gls{scRnaSeq} analysis pipeline is fundamentally constrained by the physical protocol chosen during data generation. Rather than representing a computational phase, this hardware choice establishes the data's absolute boundaries, dictating the scale, resolution, and inherent \gls{sparsity} of the resulting \gls{countMatrix}. The available protocols bifurcate into two categories with distinct computational trade-offs: plate-based methods and droplet microfluidics.

Plate-based methods, such as SMART-seq2\defn{SMART-seq2}{A plate-based full-length scRNA-seq protocol, one cell per well, capturing whole transcripts.}, physically isolate individual cells into the discrete wells of a 96- or 384-well plate. This architecture supports a capture chemistry capable of \gls{fullLength} sequencing: each transcript is captured and converted to full-length cDNA\footnote{\textbf{cDNA (complementary DNA)} is a DNA copy of an RNA molecule. Sequencers read DNA, not RNA, so the RNA must first be reverse-transcribed into cDNA before it can be read.} across its entire length, preserving the internal splice junctions necessary for direct isoform differentiation. Because each well constitutes an independent \gls{libraryPrep}, throughput is strictly bounded by reagent costs and processing labour, typically constraining experiments to a few thousand cells. In exchange, each cell receives substantial sequencing depth, yielding highly detailed per-cell profiles that make the data highly informative for isoform-level questions \cite{conte2024opportunities}.

Conversely, droplet microfluidics replace physical wells with self-assembling picolitre\defn{Picolitre}{One trillionth of a litre ($10^{-12}$\,L), roughly the volume of a sphere about $12\,\mu$m across---comparable to a single cell.} droplets. Platforms such as the 10x Genomics\defn{10x Genomics Chromium}{The dominant commercial droplet platform, barcoding each cell in a microfluidic droplet.} Chromium system co-encapsulate single cells with barcoded beads, scaling throughput to tens or hundreds of thousands of cells per run at a fraction of the cost per cell \cite{ding2020systematic}. This massive parallelisation imposes severe structural constraints on the data. Capture relies on oligo-dT\defn{Oligo-dT priming}{Capture primers made of a short chain of thymine bases, which anneal to the poly-A tail and start synthesis there. The captured fragment is therefore the 3' end of the molecule.} primers anchored to the \gls{polyATail}, restricting sequencing strictly to the $3'$ terminus\footnote{\textbf{3' (three-prime) terminus} is the end of a transcript at which the poly-A tail is added; capture chemistry anchored here reads only the terminal fragment, not the transcript body.} of each transcript. This \gls{tagBias} intrinsically discards the internal sequence structure required to resolve complex splicing events. Furthermore, a finite sequencing capacity distributed across a massive cell population drastically reduces the reads allocated per cell to a few thousand molecules, while the stochastic nature of droplet encapsulation necessitates rigorous downstream algorithmic filtering to remove empty droplets and multi-cell multiplets\defn{Multiplet}{A droplet that receives more than one cell, yielding a mixed profile that quality control must remove.}

The defining computational consequence of the droplet architecture is extreme matrix \gls{sparsity}, which follows directly from comparing the size of the reference with what a single cell yields. Let $T$ denote the number of annotated isoforms in a standard mammalian reference and $T_{obs}$ the distinct transcripts successfully recovered per single cell. While the reference dictates $T \sim 10^5$, a typical droplet-sequenced cell yields only $T_{obs} \sim 10^3$. In the raw alignment space, this disparity produces an initial matrix zero rate of roughly $99\%$. Standard preprocessing pipelines subsequently filter out features that are entirely unexpressed or ultra-rare in the sampled tissue, condensing the effective feature space down to $T_{filtered} \approx 1.2 \times 10^4$--$2.3 \times 10^4$ viable transcripts. Within this practical, filtered domain, the observed zero rate consistently settles between $70\%$ and $90\%$. 

Some of these zeros are biological zeros\defn{Biological zero}{A zero count reflecting genuine absence of a transcript in a cell, as opposed to a technical dropout.}, recording a transcript the cell never produced; the rest are \glspl{dropout}, recording a molecule that was present but never captured. Because the cumulative chemical efficiency of droplet capture, reverse transcription and sequencing succeeds on only $10\%$ to $20\%$ of the molecules present \cite{jiang2022statistics}, the second kind dominates, and the two conflate into the $70\%$ to $90\%$ zero rate reported above. They are numerically identical once recorded, so no count-based test can separate them. The $3'$ \gls{tagBias} deepens this sparsity further by collapsing distinct isoforms that share terminal exons into a single observational unit, and it is that overwhelmingly zero-heavy structure which dictates the need for a specialised statistical model in every downstream analysis.

The economics explain the adoption: a plate experiment consumes one well per cell at one to five dollars, while droplet encapsulation profiles a cell for a few cents, so a fixed budget buys two to three orders of magnitude more cells \cite{conte2024opportunities}. That trade is made knowingly. For the dominant questions---cell-type composition, marker discovery, differential gene expression---the payoff is statistical, because a per-gene test averages over thousands of cells and tolerates a sparsity that remains severe within any single one. Coverage loss is accepted wherever the question is gene-level, and resurfaces as a limitation only where it is isoform usage.

\subsection{Phase 1: Data Ingestion and Quality Control}

The first computational challenge in a single-cell pipeline is purely one of scale and structure. Sequencers output raw data in \gls{fastq} format, representing each read across four distinct lines: a coordinate identifier\defn{Read identifier}{A line beginning with \texttt{@} that names the read and records the instrument, flow cell and coordinates it came from.}, the nucleotide sequence\defn{Nucleotide sequence}{The read itself, a string of A, C, G and T bases in the order the sequencer read them.}, a separator\defn{Separator}{A line beginning with \texttt{+} that marks the end of the sequence and may repeat the identifier.}, and the corresponding Phred-scale quality scores\defn{Per-base quality score}{One character per sequenced base, encoding the probability that the base was called incorrectly on the Phred scale.}. For droplet-based chemistries such as the 10x Genomics platform, this raw sequence carries critical multiplexing metadata that must be carefully isolated before any biological analysis begins. Specifically, the sequencer must extract a \gls{cellBarcode} that maps the read back to its discrete cell of origin, and a \gls{umi}---a random tag attached prior to \gls{pcr} amplification used to track individual captured mRNA molecules.

Before the reads can be quantified, they must be cleaned. Tools such as cutadapt\defn{cutadapt}{A read-trimming tool that removes adapter sequences and low-quality bases.} or fastp\defn{fastp}{A fast all-in-one read preprocessor that performs adapter trimming, quality filtering and reporting in a single pass.} trim synthetic adapters, discard low-quality bases, and extract the barcode and UMI payloads, while heuristic filtering separates valid cell barcodes from empty droplets and multi-cell collisions (multiplets). The step is optional, and is skipped under two conditions. When a vendor pipeline such as Cell~Ranger has already trimmed adapters and corrected barcodes and UMIs, repeating the pass costs a full read of the FASTQ file without altering a base. When the mapper is read-structure-aware and UMI-aware, as alevin-fry and Salmon are, residual adapters fall outside the indexed $k$-mers and are never matched, so correction is left to the quantifier. Skipping is safe only under those guarantees: on untrimmed reads handled by a mapper that does not correct UMIs, adapters mis-assign reads and sequencing errors inflate the number of distinct UMIs. For plate-based chemistry no barcode or UMI exists to extract and adapter content is low, so trimming reduces to a quality filter.

Because quality control touches every single nucleotide exactly once, it acts as a highly parallelisable streaming operation strictly bound by the total read count, $R$, and the read length, $L$. The computational time complexity is entirely linear, scaling as:
$$ \mathcal{O}(R \cdot L) $$
The physical footprint, by contrast, is large. One uncompressed FASTQ record---a $100$\,bp read together with its identifier and quality line---occupies roughly $250$ bytes, so a droplet experiment of $R \sim 10^{9}$ reads, the order of magnitude considered here, holds $\sim 2.5 \times 10^{11}$ bytes, about $250$\,GB before compression. Modern multi-core trimmers sustain throughputs on the order of $10^{8}$ reads per minute, so this I/O-bound filtering resolves in a few hours on a single workstation.

\subsection{Phase 2: Mapping Algorithms (Alignment vs.\ Pseudo-Alignment)}

Quantifying transcript expression is fundamentally a large-scale assignment problem: mapping short sequencing reads back to their reference transcripts of origin. The computational workload is strictly governed by the total read count, which scales as the product of the cell population and the sequencing depth. This relationship is defined mathematically as:
$$ R = N \cdot M $$
where $R$ is the total read count, $N$ is the number of cells, and $M$ is the average number of reads per cell. Both bounds below are derived with $R$ symbolic and instantiated only once, here. The two datasets analysed in this thesis total roughly $10^{8}$--$10^{9}$ reads, so $R \sim 10^{9}$ is the scale used throughout; pooled multi-sample atlases reach $10^{10}$--$10^{11}$ reads and scale every cost in this section by the same factor. The search space and query geometry impose additional dimensional constraints, defined by the mammalian reference transcriptome and the physical sequencer output respectively:
$$ T \sim 10^5, \qquad L_{avg} \approx 1500\,\text{bp}, \qquad L \approx 90\text{--}150\,\text{bp} $$
where $T$ denotes the total number of annotated isoforms (including unspliced pre-mRNA), $L_{avg}$ the average transcript length in base pairs, and $L$ the sequenced read length in base pairs. Read length is strictly a hardware constraint; in a $3'$ assay, Read~1 isolates the cell barcode and UMI, leaving solely Read~2 to capture the actual transcript sequence.

Traditional alignment algorithms\defn{Read alignment}{Base-by-base mapping of a read to the reference, giving exact coordinates at high computational cost.} (e.g., BWA \cite{li2009bwa}, Bowtie2 \cite{langmead2012bowtie2}) evaluate this mapping base-by-base to derive exact genomic coordinates using a Burrows-Wheeler Transform\defn{Burrows-Wheeler Transform (BWT)}{A reversible text transform that allows fast substring search in a compressed reference index.} index. Building this index requires a one-time computational investment proportional to the entire transcriptome size, scaling as $\mathcal{O}(T \cdot L_{avg})$. However, during the query phase, each of the $R$ reads is localised through a search algorithm whose time cost grows logarithmically with the size of the reference $T$. Factoring in the algorithm's tolerance for sequencing errors, insertions, and deletions, this produces a total execution time of:
$$ \mathcal{O}(T \cdot L_{avg}) + \mathcal{O}(R \cdot L \log T) $$
Searching the index does not scan the reference: backward search extends the matched suffix one base at a time, at constant cost per base. The logarithmic term above instead reflects the branch-and-bound over candidate loci that error tolerance requires, since a reference holding more transcripts offers more positions to test. That search penalty is what makes exhaustive alignment computationally prohibitive across the billions of reads generated in a single-cell experiment.

Modern droplet-based transcriptomics circumvents this bottleneck by utilising pseudo-alignment algorithms, such as Salmon, alevin-fry, or kallisto \cite{patro2017salmon, bray2016near, he2022alevin}. This approach rests on the critical mathematical observation that downstream quantification requires only the identity of the transcript of origin, not the exact base-level coordinates of the read. Consequently, pseudo-alignment replaces logarithmic sequence searches entirely with constant-time hash table lookups. Each read is fragmented into its overlapping $k$-mers\defn{k-mer}{A contiguous substring of length $k$; reads are matched to transcripts by the $k$-mers they share.}, and every $k$-mer is queried in a pre-computed table recording which transcripts contain it. Intersecting these sets rapidly yields an equivalence class\defn{Equivalence class}{The set of transcripts that could have produced a read, since all contain the read's $k$-mers.} of candidate transcripts compatible with the read \cite{he2022alevin}.

The procedure executes in two distinct computational phases. First, during index construction, the reference transcriptome is decomposed into an index---typically a colored de Bruijn graph\defn{Colored de Bruijn graph}{A graph of overlapping $k$-mers in which each $k$-mer is coloured by the transcripts containing it, used as the pseudo-alignment index.} mapping each $k$-mer to the specific transcripts containing it. This incurs a time cost identical to traditional alignment, but the index is stored sparsely: only the $k$-mers actually present in the reference are represented, giving a spatial footprint of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ for $d$ distinct observed $k$-mers. The dense worst case, $\mathcal{O}(k \cdot |\Sigma|^k)$ over the alphabet of size $|\Sigma| = 4$, is $4^{31} \approx 4.6 \times 10^{18}$ entries and is never materialised. Like traditional alignment, this index is built exactly once before any read is processed.

Second, during the query phase, each read is fragmented into its $L - k + 1$ overlapping $k$-mers. The algorithm executes a hash table lookup for every single $k$-mer, retrieving and intersecting their corresponding transcript sets to produce the equivalence class. With a standard $k = 31$ and $L \approx 90\text{--}150$\,bp, this requires roughly $60$ to $120$ lookups per read. By shifting the core operation from a base-by-base sequence search to a hash retrieval, the per-read query cost collapses to $\mathcal{O}(L)$, completely decoupling it from the massive size of the reference transcriptome. The total execution time for pseudo-alignment therefore simplifies to:
$$ \mathcal{O}(T \cdot L_{avg}) + \mathcal{O}(R \cdot L) $$

Instantiating the two query terms at $R \sim 10^{9}$ reads of length $L \sim 10^{2}$\,bp against $T \sim 10^{5}$ transcripts, where $\log_2 T \approx 16.6$:
$$ R \cdot L \log_2 T \approx 10^{9} \cdot 10^{2} \cdot 16.6 \approx 1.7 \times 10^{12}, \qquad R \cdot L \approx 10^{9} \cdot 10^{2} = 1.0 \times 10^{11} $$
Two separate effects account for the gap. Pseudo-alignment removes the asymptotic $\log_2 T \approx 16.6$ factor, which is worth roughly one order of magnitude on its own. The remainder of the reported $50$- to $100$-fold speed-up comes from the constant factor: retrieving a hash-table entry costs far less than an inexact string match. The two effects multiply, and together they turn a query phase measured in days into one measured in hours.

\subsection{Phase 3: Probabilistic Resolution via Expectation-Maximization}

Pseudo-alignment introduces a distinct computational challenge in the form of multi-mapping reads\defn{Multi-mapping read}{A read compatible with more than one transcript because those transcripts share its sequence.}---reads whose $k$-mers match more than one transcript because those transcripts naturally share exonic sequences. A read falling entirely within a shared exon carries no intrinsic sequence information about which specific isoform it originated from, prompting the pseudo-aligner to return a set of valid candidates rather than a single definitive transcript. This ambiguity is profoundly exacerbated in \gls{threePrime} protocols, where short reads capture exclusively the terminal region and frequently match every single isoform sharing that specific terminal exon \cite{galfre2021cotan}.

Discarding these ambiguous reads is statistically unviable. Because they form the overwhelming majority of alignments in \gls{threePrime} data, summarily dropping them would severely deplete the count matrix and artificially bias isoform proportions toward rare variants that happen to carry unique sequences.

Modern pseudo-aligners instead resolve this ambiguity by casting quantification as maximum-likelihood estimation over a generative model of the sequencing experiment \cite{patro2017salmon}. Each fragment is assumed to be produced in two stages: a transcript $t$ is first drawn with probability proportional to its relative abundance $\theta_t$, and a start position is then drawn uniformly from the $\ell_t$ positions at which a fragment of the observed length can begin. The admissible count $\ell_t$ is the transcript's \defn{Effective length}{The number of fragment start positions a transcript can generate; approximately its length minus the fragment length, plus one. It corrects for the fact that longer transcripts yield more fragments at equal abundance.}. Crucially, the transcript of origin is latent: pseudo-alignment reports only the equivalence class $C_r$ of transcripts compatible with read $r$ \cite{bray2016near}. Marginalising over the unobserved origin gives the probability of observing that class under a given abundance vector:
$$ P(C_r \mid \theta) = \frac{\sum_{t \in C_r} \theta_t \, \ell_t}{\sum_{t'} \theta_{t'} \, \ell_{t'}}, $$
and the quantity to be maximised is the product of these terms over all reads:
$$ \hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta} \prod_{r} P(C_r \mid \theta), \qquad \sum_{t} \theta_t = 1. $$
Because the origin of each read is unobserved, this likelihood is optimised by the Expectation-Maximization (EM)\defn{Expectation-Maximization (EM)}{An iterative algorithm that estimates transcript abundances and resolves ambiguous read assignments jointly until they converge.} algorithm, which alternates between two phases until convergence.

In the E-step\defn{E-step / M-step}{The two alternating EM phases: distribute reads by the current abundance estimate (E), then re-estimate abundances (M).}, the current estimate is used to compute the posterior probability that read $r$ originated from transcript $t$, given that it originated within the class $C_r$:
$$ \hat{z}_{rt} = \frac{\theta_t \, \ell_t}{\sum_{t' \in C_r} \theta_{t'} \, \ell_{t'}} \quad \text{for } t \in C_r, $$
and $\hat{z}_{rt} = 0$ otherwise. The effective-length term $\ell_t$ is not optional: it encodes the fact that a long transcript contributes proportionally more fragments at equal abundance, so omitting it biases $\hat{\theta}$ toward short isoforms. The algorithm then proceeds to the M-step, where each abundance is re-estimated as the total posterior weight assigned to its transcript:
$$ \theta_t \propto \sum_{r \,:\, t \in C_r} \hat{z}_{rt}, $$
normalised so that the values of $\theta_t$ sum to exactly one across the transcriptome.

Two properties of this iteration matter for the interpretation of the resulting counts. First, each round is guaranteed not to decrease the log-likelihood, so the estimates converge monotonically to a \emph{local} optimum rather than a guaranteed global one \cite{dempster1977maximum}. The likelihood surface is frequently flat or multimodal, so implementations initialise $\theta$ uniformly, stop when the relative change in log-likelihood falls below a tolerance, and may return different solutions for different starting points. Second, the model is closed-world: every read is assumed to originate from an annotated transcript, so an incomplete reference, unannotated isoforms or uncorrected fragment biases propagate directly into $\hat{\theta}$. The estimate is consequently a point estimate without uncertainty, and downstream methods that require variance---particularly the DTU tools discussed later---recover it by bootstrapping the quantification step \cite{love2018swimming}.

Because the summation runs over equivalence classes rather than individual reads, many reads that share a compatible transcript set are handled by a single update. The computational cost therefore scales with the number of \emph{distinct} classes, not with the number of reads, which is what makes transcriptome-wide EM tractable. Alevin-fry's parsimonious variant exploits this further by collapsing transcripts that share an identical set of $k$-mers into a single group and running the EM updates over those groups, effectively degrading the problem to gene-level quantification where isoform resolution is impossible \cite{he2022alevin}. This is precisely the regime that \gls{threePrime} data tends to occupy, and it is worth stating why it cannot be repaired by more data.

\paragraph{Remark (identifiability under $3'$ capture).}
Let $t$ and $t'$ be two isoforms of the same gene whose reads fall only in equivalence classes containing both, as happens when the shared terminal region covers their effective lengths. The E-step above then assigns posterior weight through the ratio $\theta_t \ell_t / (\theta_t \ell_t + \theta_{t'} \ell_{t'})$ alone. With $\ell_t \approx \ell_{t'}$ that ratio reduces to $\theta_t / (\theta_t + \theta_{t'})$, so the likelihood depends on the two abundances only through their sum and is flat along $\theta_t + \theta_{t'} = \text{const}$: every split that preserves the total explains the observed reads equally well. Identifiability is therefore bounded by the divergence of the terminal region, not by the estimator, the reference or the number of reads, since sequencing the same fragment more deeply cannot separate two transcripts that fragment cannot distinguish. This is the formal reason the transcript-level analysis of this thesis can recover only those switches whose isoforms differ at or near their $3'$ ends.

\subsection{Phase 4: Deduplication and Matrix Generation}

Once each read has been assigned to a transcript, the remaining task is to count molecules rather than reads. The protocol attaches a \gls{umi}---a short random sequence---to each captured molecule before amplification, so every read carries both a \gls{cellBarcode} identifying its cell and a UMI identifying its molecule. Because PCR copies inherit both tags, the reads descending from one molecule are indistinguishable duplicates, and collapsing each such group recovers the molecule counts that sequencing had inflated.

The collapse must be performed on barcode, UMI \emph{and} assigned transcript together. A UMI holds only a few random bases, so within one cell two unrelated molecules can share a UMI by chance; grouping on barcode and UMI alone would merge them, whereas including the transcript keeps them apart whenever they originate from different features.

The result is the expression count matrix $X$ (Table~\ref{tab:pipeline-phases}), whose entry $x_{fc}$ records the number of distinct molecules observed for feature $f$ in cell $c$. Amplification no longer distorts the counts, so the differences remaining between cells reflect biology and capture efficiency rather than how often each molecule happened to be copied.

\section{COTAN Framework}\label{sec:cotan-framework}

The transcript-level matrix $X$ produced above acts as the natural input for the statistical framework presented here, yet it simultaneously introduces the primary analytical difficulty: the shallow per-cell depth heavily imposed by the droplet hardware leaves the vast majority of its entries at zero. The COTAN framework, introduced by Galfr\`e et al.\ \cite{galfre2021cotan}, was specifically engineered to analyse this zero-heavy structure directly, and has since been logically extended to recover entire cell clusters purely from co-expression patterns \cite{galfre2026gene}. The following subsections formalise this sparsity and detail the contingency-table machinery COTAN employs to successfully bypass traditional density-based statistics.

\subsection{The Sparsity Problem}

Single-cell count matrices exhibit extreme structural sparsity, with $70\%$ to $90\%$ of all entries returning zero \cite{galfre2021cotan}. As noted when the matrix was introduced, the biological and technical origins of those zeros are indistinguishable once recorded: a transcript that was truly absent and one that was merely missed leave the same mark. That ambiguity, rather than the sparsity itself, is the fundamental statistical barrier for conventional tools designed for deep, dense \gls{bulkRnaSeq} data.

Applying standard density-based statistical methods to such sparse matrices introduces severe, compounding computational artifacts. Log-normalisation, which strictly involves adding a pseudo-count before logarithmic scaling, heavily distorts low-abundance measurements and artificially compresses legitimate zeros into spurious scaling patterns \cite{galfre2021cotan}. Standard linear or rank-based association measures, such as Pearson or Spearman correlations, fail entirely in this domain, as they inevitably end up measuring the coincidence of technical dropouts rather than identifying true regulatory co-expression \cite{galfre2021cotan}. Furthermore, attempting to artificially densify the matrix through heuristic imputation---specifically replacing zeros with smoothed estimates derived from neighbouring cells---frequently introduces fabricated correlations and severely compromises sensitive downstream signals \cite{galfre2021cotan}. To fundamentally circumvent these pitfalls, COTAN explicitly abandons imputation and variance-stabilising transformations entirely, choosing instead to evaluate the zeros as a direct signal through strict binary discretisation.

\subsection{Contingency Table Solution}

COTAN cleanly bypasses the flawed continuous count amplitudes by evaluating pairwise feature dependencies using asymptotic independence testing applied strictly to binary presence and absence states \cite{galfre2021cotan}. For any given feature pair $(f, f')$ across $N$ cells, the continuous counts are logically reduced to discrete indicator variables for each cell $c$:
$$ a_c = \mathbf{1}[x_{fc} > 0], \qquad b_c = \mathbf{1}[x_{f'c} > 0] $$
Letting the discrete states be indexed by $i, j \in \{0,1\}$, the framework constructs a comprehensive $2 \times 2$ \gls{contingencyTable} where $O_{ij}$ directly denotes the observed number of cells exhibiting state $a_c = i$ and $b_c = j$. The marginal totals for this specific table are defined as $\mathrm{Row}_i = \sum_j O_{ij}$ and $\mathrm{Col}_j = \sum_i O_{ij}$, mathematically constrained by the total cell count $\sum_i \mathrm{Row}_i = \sum_j \mathrm{Col}_j = N$. Under the standard null hypothesis of statistical independence, the expected cell count $E_{ij}$ is calculated directly as:
$$ E_{ij} = \frac{\mathrm{Row}_i \cdot \mathrm{Col}_j}{N} $$

Table~\ref{tab:contingency} lays the four counts out explicitly. The two diagonal cells hold cells in which the features are detected together ($O_{11}$) or neither is detected ($O_{00}$); the off-diagonal cells hold cells defining the difference between them, in which exactly one of the two is present. Co-expression resides in the tendency of the count to fall on a diagonal rather than on the anti-diagonal, and the expected value $E_{ij}$ above is what that count would be under independence.

Deviation from this independent expectation is evaluated using Pearson's chi-squared statistic \cite{galfre2021cotan}:
$$ \chi^2 = \sum_{i \in \{0,1\}} \sum_{j \in \{0,1\}} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} $$

The result is reported not as a statistic but as a single coefficient. COTAN defines the co-expression coefficient $C_{f,f'}$ by rescaling the deviation so that it lies in $(-1, 1)$ and, under the null hypothesis, satisfies $N\,C_{f,f'}^2 \sim \chi^2(1)$ over the $N$ cells of the table. The pairwise $p$-value, the quantity used for screening throughout this thesis, then follows directly:
$$ p_{f,f'} = P\!\left(\chi^2(1) > N\,C_{f,f'}^2\right) $$
A positive coefficient means the two features are detected together more often than independence predicts; a negative one means less often, which is the mutual exclusivity that Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} treats as the signal of interest.

One modification separates $C_{f,f'}$ from the textbook Phi coefficient of a $2 \times 2$ table, and it lies in the expectation rather than in the statistic. Taking $E_{ij}$ proportionally to the marginals, as above, assumes that every cell has the same chance of expressing a given feature. Cells differ widely in library size, so the cells that capture less RNA accumulate zeros across all features at once, and a proportional expectation would read that shared under-detection as co-expression and inflate the coefficient. COTAN therefore derives the expected co-occurrence from a model of the zeros themselves: feature $g$ in cell $c$ is assigned a negative-binomial probability of a zero count with mean $\lambda_g \nu_c$ and shape $\alpha_g$, where $\lambda_g$ is the feature's average expression, $\nu_c$ scales with the library size of cell $c$, and $\alpha_g$ is calibrated from the number of zeros actually observed for that feature \cite{galfre2021cotan}. Co-expression is thus measured against how often two features' zeros were expected to coincide, not against their raw marginals.

The chi-squared approximation behind $p_{f,f'}$ is asymptotic in the number of cells, not in the counts, and the distinction matters here. With $N$ in the thousands the calibration is reliable for features detected in a reasonable fraction of cells, which covers most of a standard gene matrix. It degrades in the opposite regime, where a feature is rare enough that several expected cell counts $E_{ij}$ fall below about five; Pearson's statistic is then anti-conservative, and such a pair is best read as inconclusive rather than significant. Filtering cannot remove this effect for the rarest transcripts, which is why candidates are ultimately ranked by effect size rather than by $p$-value alone.

A gene carrying $k$ detected isoforms also contributes $\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise tests, and these are screened at a nominal $\alpha = 0.05$ with no correction applied. Since the tests are counted within genes rather than across the whole matrix, the adjustment a stricter procedure would impose is modest, and the effect-size threshold described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} provides the practical control. A permutation-calibrated null would be more principled and is noted as such in the discussion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Output Metrics}

The framework reduces the machinery above to three output metrics \cite{galfre2021cotan}, each of which is used later in this thesis. The first is the co-expression matrix $C$, whose entries are the coefficients $C_{f,f'}$ just defined; its negative values denote mutual exclusivity, and its positive values co-expression.

The second is differential expression analysis (DEA), which asks whether a single feature $f$ is enriched in a given cell cluster $S$. It is the same construction applied to a different table: cells inside or outside $S$ are crossed with feature $f$ detected or not, and the resulting correlation coefficient $D_{f,S} \in (-1, 1)$ reports the association. A positive $D_{f,S}$ means $f$ is detected more often inside $S$ than outside it, a negative one less often. Because every DEA coefficient shares one bounded scale, values are comparable across features and across clusters, which is what allows the DTU contrast of Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} to be formed as the difference of two of them.

The third is the \gls{gdi}, a per-feature cluster-specificity measure. For feature $i$ it takes the mean of the smallest $5\%$ of its pairwise $p$-values against all other features, $\bar{p}_i$, and applies $\mathrm{gdi}_i = \ln(-\ln \bar{p}_i)$, which compresses the distribution of those means into an interpretable range \cite{galfre2021cotan}. Constitutively expressed features produce an approximately uniform lower tail and cluster around the null median $\ln(-\ln 0.025) \approx 1.305$, whereas features that vary across cell sub-populations accumulate very small $p$-values and rise above it. Values beyond roughly $1.5$ therefore indicate features worth following up with DEA, and the GDI is used in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} as the check that a clustering has recovered real population structure rather than noise.

Within transcript-level analysis the pairwise coefficient carries a sharper meaning than it does between genes. Observing a strong negative $C_{f,f'}$ between two isoforms of the same parent gene is the primary statistical indicator of alternative transcript usage, since it states that the two are detected in different cells more often than chance allows.

\section{DTU Fundamentals}

\subsection{DTU vs Differential Gene Expression}

Differential Transcript Usage (\gls{dtu}) occurs distinctly when the relative expression proportions of multiple transcripts originating from the exact same parent gene change significantly between different biological conditions or cellular states. This concept differs fundamentally from Differential Gene Expression (\gls{deg}), which strictly measures overall changes in total, aggregated gene abundance without regard for internal composition. Biologically, DTU physically reflects shifts in \gls{splicing} decisions---a critical regulatory mechanism enabling cells to produce functionally distinct protein isoforms from an identical genomic sequence. Detecting these precise regulatory shifts inherently requires transcript-level resolution, as aggregating raw counts to the broader gene level mathematically obliterates the nuanced isoform switching signal entirely.

\subsection{Why Bulk Methods Fail on scRNA-seq}

Established bulk RNA-seq DTU methodologies, such as rMATS, SUPPA2, and DRIMSeq, are structurally engineered to model continuous isoform proportions by relying heavily on deep, internal, junction-spanning read coverage. Consequently, these tools fail catastrophically when applied directly to single-cell data due to three compounding factors \cite{galfre2021cotan, love2018swimming}. First, the exceptionally low per-cell sequencing depth produces highly degenerate proportion estimates overwhelmingly dominated by zeros. Second, the $3'$-bias inherent to droplet protocols renders internal alternative splicing events entirely invisible to traditional junction-spanning algorithms. Finally, pseudo-bulk aggregation recovers the required depth only by discarding the cell-to-cell variation the assay exists to measure \cite{gilis2021satuRn, tekath2021dturtle}.

While proportion-based DTU methods have been extended to single-cell paradigms, they remain proportion models: the depth they require is still recovered by aggregation, or depends on junction-spanning coverage the droplet chemistry never provides. As surveyed by Draper et al.\ \cite{draper2025selecting}, none of these contemporary approaches exploits the zero-heavy matrix pattern as a direct source of analytical signal. That gap provides the core motivation for adapting COTAN's sparsity-robust contingency framework to transcript-level analysis.

\subsection{Mutual Exclusivity Signal}\label{sec:mutual-exclusivity}

Within the adapted COTAN framework, negative statistical associations---formally defined as \gls{mutualExclusivity}---between features serve as the foundational indicator of alternative usage patterns. When applied specifically to multiple isoforms of the exact same parent gene, this structural mutual exclusivity is biologically consistent with alternative splicing or \gls{isoformSwitch} events, wherein different cellular states preferentially express entirely distinct transcript variants.

However, mutual exclusivity in isolation is mathematically insufficient to prove a functional switch. Two isoforms may yield a mutually exclusive signature simply because one variant is narrowly confined to a single specific cluster while the other is broadly ubiquitously expressed. This pattern of one-sided enrichment is biologically consistent with tissue-restricted isoform expression, but does not strictly reflect an active regulatory switch in splicing decisions. Identifying a genuine isoform switch requires explicitly demonstrating bidirectional opposition: establishing at least one specific cell cluster in which the first isoform is heavily preferred, and at least one other distinct cluster in which that preference explicitly flips to its partner. This bidirectional contrast serves as the definitive conceptual filter mathematically separating a true \gls{dtu} event from simple one-sided enrichment. While the present chapter successfully establishes mutual exclusivity as the core statistical signal worth testing, the precise mathematical metric that formalises this bidirectional contrast requirement is introduced and explicitly defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:methods}.
