Chapter 1

Introduction
1.1

Context and Motivation

In single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), standard Differential Expression (DE)
analysis typically evaluates expression totals at the gene level[cite: 1]. However, gene-level aggregation masks transcriptomic diversity, as many genes encode multiple distinct functional products through alternative transcripts or isoforms[cite: 1]. Differential Transcript Usage (DTU) analysis investigates whether
the relative proportions of these isoforms shift between experimental conditions,
cell types, or along a developmental trajectory[cite: 1]. Crucially, DTU can occur
even when overall gene expression remains constant[cite: 1].

1.2

The Computational Challenge

Recovering DTU from scRNA-seq data is notoriously challenging due to the structural properties of the resulting count matrices[cite: 1]:
• Extreme Sparsity: Single-cell count matrices are predominantly zero-inflated,
with zero entries typically exceeding 70–90%[cite: 1].
• Positional Bias: The data is predominantly 3′ -biased, as standard highthroughput droplet-based assays capture primarily transcript ends rather
than full-length sequences[cite: 1].
This positional bias is the result of a strict technical trade-off: “full-length”
sequencing protocols capture entire transcript bodies but are expensive and lowthroughput, whereas droplet-based protocols prioritize scale, yielding high statistical power across tens of thousands of cells at the cost of capturing only transcript
termini[cite: 1]. Consequently, resolving transcript isoforms at scale requires algorithms capable of functioning within heavily constrained coverage[cite: 1]. Because existing DTU algorithms were designed for the dense, continuous data of
2

bulk RNA-seq, they encounter degenerate solutions when applied under these
severe sparsity and coverage constraints[cite: 1].

1.3

Thesis Objective

The goal of this thesis is to recover Differential Transcript Usage (DTU) from
sparse, 3′ -biased scRNA-seq data[cite: 1]. This is achieved by adapting the COTAN
(Co-expression Tables Analysis) framework to operate at transcript-level resolution[cite: 1]. COTAN was specifically selected for this task because it is designed
to handle extremely sparse data effectively; by modeling zero-inflated distributions as an informational signal rather than noise, it has the potential to retain
critical transcript-level variation that standard methods discard[cite: 1].

1.4

Thesis Outline

• Chapter 2 reviews the mathematical challenges of matrix sparsity and the
statistical foundations of the COTAN framework[cite: 1].
• Chapter 3 details the reproducible methodology, including the custom pipeline
for transcript matrix construction and the DTU detection algorithm[cite: 1].
• Chapter 4 evaluates the empirical clustering and DTU discovery results
across human and mouse datasets[cite: 1].
• Chapter 5 summarizes the methodological contributions and outlines future computational extensions[cite: 1].

Chapter 2

Background and
Fundamentals
To adapt the COTAN framework for transcript-level analysis, it is necessary to
first define the computational properties and statistical constraints of single-cell
data[cite: 1].

2.1

The scRNA-seq Count Matrix and the Sparsity
Problem

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) profiles gene expression at the resolution of individual cells. From a computational perspective, the output is an expression count matrix X ∈ NG×C , where rows index genomic features (genes
or transcripts), columns represent distinct single cells, and entry xij records the
observed read count for feature i in cell j .
The final output is a sparse digital representation of the transcriptome. Singlecell count matrices are overwhelmingly sparse, with zero values typically exceeding 70–90% of all entries. This sparsity is the central computational challenge in
scRNA-seq analysis.
Zero entries fall into two indistinguishable categories:
• Biological Zeros: The feature is genuinely not expressed in that specific
cell state.
• Technical Zeros (Dropouts): The feature was present but failed to be captured or sequenced due to low chemical efficiency (typically 10–20%) or
stochastic sampling.
Standard statistical pipelines developed for bulk data fail under extreme sparsity because their mathematical assumptions are violated:
4

1. Variance-Stabilizing Transformations: Pseudo-count log transformations,
such as log(x + c), introduce arbitrary scale artifacts when applied to zeroinflated data.
2. Linear Correlation Metrics: Pearson and Spearman correlations assume
continuous distributions; in sparse matrices, they are heavily distorted by
the joint occurrence of technical zeros.
3. Imputation: Algorithmic guessing of missing values smooths out zeros but
often introduces artificial correlations and false signals.
Robust inference requires statistical frameworks that treat zero observations
not as missing data to be patched, but as explicit parameters in a probability distribution.

2.2

count amplitudes and instead evaluates pairwise dependencies using asymptotic
independence testing on binary presence/absence states[cite: 1].

into a 2×2 contingency table[cite: 1]. Under the null hypothesis (H0 ) of statistical
independence, the expected joint frequency Eij is defined as:

2.3

Transcript Quantification and Aggregation

To generate the initial count matrix, modern pipelines use pseudo-alignment algorithms (such as alevin-fry) to probabilistically map raw sequencing reads
5

directly to specific transcript isoforms[cite: 1]. While these algorithms naturally
calculate expression at the transcript resolution, standard scRNA-seq workflows
routinely sum these individual transcript counts together into a single, aggregated
gene-level count[cite: 1].
This conventional aggregation step is performed to reduce technical noise and
matrix dimensionality[cite: 1]. However, it irreversibly destroys the isoform-level
variation necessary for detecting DTU[cite: 1]. Therefore, recovering DTU requires intercepting the pipeline before aggregation to preserve the uncollapsed
transcript-level count matrix[cite: 1].

2.4

Prior Work on DTU Detection

In bulk RNA-seq, established frameworks successfully detect DTU by modeling
continuous proportions[cite: 1]. However, translating these methods to singlecell data is impeded by low per-cell read depth (where continuous proportion
models break down) and terminal positional bias (which renders internal splicing
events invisible)[cite: 1]. Furthermore, aggregating cells into pseudo-bulk profiles
to recover read depth obscures the cell-to-cell heterogeneity that drives DTU[cite:
1].

2.5

The COTAN Analytical Framework

The COTAN (Co-expression Tables Analysis) framework is designed to model
sparsity directly[cite: 1]. By evaluating binary occurrence patterns rather than
continuous counts, COTAN is robust to sequencing depth variations[cite: 1]. In
the context of transcripts, highly negative statistical association (mutual exclusivity) between two isoforms of the same gene is a primary mathematical indicator
of alternative transcript usage[cite: 1].
COTAN generates three primary metrics[cite: 1]:
1. Co-expression Matrix (coex): Measures normalized pairwise associations[cite:
1].
2. Differential Expression Analysis (DEA): Quantifies feature detection probabilities across cellular subpopulations[cite: 1].
3. Global Differentiation Index (GDI): Identifies condition-specific markers
by measuring how specifically a feature is expressed relative to the rest of
the dataset[cite: 1].
In summary, because current DTU algorithms fail on droplet scRNA-seq data
due to matrix sparsity, this thesis adapts the COTAN framework to transcriptlevel matrices to identify mutually exclusive isoform pairs without requiring artificial imputation or gene-level aggregation[cite: 1].

Chapter 3

Materials and Methods
This chapter documents every methodological choice made in this thesis. The
goal is transparency: an expert should be able to read these decisions, understand the rationale, and judge whether they would make different choices. Reproducibility requires documenting not just what was done, but why alternatives
were rejected.

3.1

Dataset Selection Strategy

The foundation of any computational analysis is appropriate data selection. Poor
quality or mismatched datasets cannot be rescued by sophisticated algorithms
downstream. Our strategy prioritized three criteria: known ground-truth biology,
3′ -biased sequencing chemistry (matching real-world constraints), and sufficient
cell numbers for statistical power.

Why COTAN Datasets analysis?
Rather than searching the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) ad hoc, we started
from the curated collection at https://seriph78.github.io/COTAN Datasets
analysis/. This resource offers several advantages:
• Pre-validated quality: These datasets were used in COTAN’s original benchmarking papers, confirming they work with the framework and contain no
major technical artifacts.
• Known ground truth: Cell line mixtures have documented identities (e.g.,
A549 cells express EGFR mutations), enabling validation that clustering recovers expected biology.
• Standardized preprocessing: Many datasets come with published barcodes for high-quality cells, reducing the risk of including low-quality droplets
that would confound downstream analysis.
7

This choice trades breadth (fewer total options than raw GEO search) for
reliability—critical when testing whether a novel algorithm actually detects biological signal rather than technical noise.

Requirement: 3′ -Biased Sequencing Data
Our objective is to test DTU detection under realistic constraints, not ideal conditions. This means intentionally choosing the harder problem:
• Full-length protocols (SMART-seq2) capture entire transcripts, making
isoform distinction relatively straightforward with standard tools.
• 3′ -biased droplet methods (10x Genomics) sequence only transcript ends,
where many alternative splicing events are invisible. Success here would
demonstrate genuine robustness.
By choosing 3′ -biased data, we establish a lower bound on COTAN’s DTU
detection capability. If it works under these constraints, the method has practical
value for large-scale studies where cost demands droplet-based sequencing.

Absence of Published Transcript Matrices
A critical obstacle emerged during the dataset search: no public scRNA-seq
transcript-level count matrices exist. This is not an oversight—it reflects standard practice in the field:
• Most pipelines (CellRanger, Seurat’s recommended workflow) collapse to
gene counts by default.
• Transcript quantification requires additional computational steps (pseudoalignment with Salmon/alevin-fry) that many analysts skip.
• Even when transcript-level analysis is performed, results are typically aggregated back to genes before publication.
The consequence: we must build transcript matrices from FASTQ files ourselves. This adds computational overhead but also provides complete control over
the quantification process—a reproducibility advantage once documented properly.

We chose alevin-fry (simpleaf mode, part of Salmon) for three reasons:
1. Superior UMI handling: Single-cell data uses Unique Molecular Identifiers to correct PCR amplification bias. The alevin-fry error model better
distinguishes true UMIs from sequencing errors.
2. Multi-mapping resolution: Reads compatible with multiple transcripts
are assigned probabilistically via Expectation-Maximization (EM), rather than
uniformly split. This preserves relative abundance signals for downstream
analysis.
3. nf-core integration: The nf-core/scrnaseq pipeline (v4.2.0) has native support for simpleaf with fewer configuration quirks than kallisto alternatives.
The EM algorithm choice matters: we used parsimony-em, which assigns
multi-mapping reads to the minimal set of transcripts consistent with observed
data. This produces fractional counts that must be rounded (see Section 3.2).

The Modification Process (Reproducible Steps)
Standard alevin-fry output collapses to gene level by default via transcript-togene mapping. To extract transcript counts, we modified the index files before
alignment:
1. Replace t2g.txt with an identity mapping:

# Original: ENSMUST00000193814 ENSMUSG00000067548 # transcript -¿ gene
# Modified: ENSMUST00000193814 ENSMUST00000193814 # transcript -¿ itse

This prevents the final aggregation step that sums isoforms into genes.
9

2. Remove gene id to names.txt: The nf-core pipeline expects this file
for annotation. Deleting it causes a controlled exit at the annotation stage,
after transcript counts have already been written to disk and can be salvaged.
3. Run parsimony-em with rounding: EM produces fractional assignments
(e.g., isoform A: 12.7 reads, isoform B: 3.4 reads). Since COTAN requires integer counts, we round to nearest integers. This preserves total UMI counts
while maintaining relative isoform proportions.
This modification is reversible: restoring standard index files returns genelevel quantification without re-running alignment. It adds minimal computational
overhead (index editing takes seconds) while enabling transcript-resolution output from a pipeline designed for genes.

Custom Nextflow Pipeline
To make this workflow reproducible across datasets, we developed a custom Nextflow
automation script:
• Input: List of SRR accessions and genome reference (FASTA and GTF).
• Automated steps:
1. Download FASTQ files via sra-tools.
2. Build or reuse transcriptome index with salmon.
3. Apply t2g.txt modification if transcript-level output is requested.
4. Execute nf-core/scrnaseq (v4.2.0) with simpleaf.
5. Format outputs for COTAN ingestion (matrix and metadata).
• GitHub repository: https://github.com/TODO/GITHUB LINK. The
pipeline includes documentation, example configuration files, and test cases.
This automation reduces the analysis from a multi-day manual process to a
single command-line invocation. Future researchers can apply it to new datasets
without re-implementing the workflow.

3.3

Data Filtering Choices

Raw count matrices contain noise that must be removed before statistical analysis.
Our filtering strategy prioritizes biological signal over maximizing cell counts,
accepting smaller but higher-quality final datasets.
10

Cell-level Filters
We filtered cells using metadata from the original GEO publications:
• Matching barcodes to published high-quality sets: The Arrigoni dataset
(GSE243665) and Ding dataset (GSE132044) both provide lists of cells that
passed their quality control thresholds. By restricting analysis to these knowngood barcodes, we avoid implementing our own QC pipeline that might introduce inconsistencies.
• Rationale: Published studies typically filter for:
– Minimum UMI counts per cell (removes empty droplets).
– Maximum mitochondrial read percentage (removes dying cells).
– Gene detection thresholds (cells with too few genes may be doublets or
low quality).
Reusing their filtering ensures comparability and reduces methodological
drift.
Final cell counts: 29k human (Arrigoni), 4k mouse (Ding).

Feature-level Filters
Transcript matrices contain many features that add noise without signal:
1. Remove unspliced transcripts: COTAN’s framework was designed for
mature mRNA. Unspliced pre-mRNA (nascent transcription) has different
statistical properties and can confound co-expression analysis. We filtered
to spliced-only features, reducing matrix dimensionality.
2. Ultra-rare isoform cutoff: Transcripts detected in fewer than 3 cells across
the entire dataset contribute mostly zeros to contingency tables. These
sparse features inflate computational cost without improving statistical power.
Removing them reduces false positives from spurious associations.
3. Final matrix dimensions:
• Human: 29k cells × 23k transcripts (after filtering).
• Mouse: 4k cells × 12k transcripts.
These choices prioritize quality over quantity. Smaller matrices mean faster
computation and cleaner signal—critical when running expensive contingency
table calculations across all feature pairs.

3.4

Clustering and Validation Strategy

A central question in this thesis is whether transcript-level data captures cellular
structure that gene-level analysis misses. To answer this, we implemented a threepart clustering strategy: primary analysis on transcripts, validation against genelevel baselines, and comparison of DTU detection across both approaches.

Transcript-level Clustering (Primary Analysis)
The main analysis clusters cells using the transcript count matrix:
• Algorithm: COTAN’s hierarchical uniform clustering.
• Procedure:
1. Compute cell-cell dissimilarity based on co-expression patterns.
2. Recursively split clusters until reaching homogeneous groups (no further significant structure).
3. Merge adjacent clusters that are statistically indistinguishable.
• Output for mouse dataset: 11 uniform clusters plus a small noisy remainder.
This clustering represents the hypothesis: if transcript-level data captures
meaningful biological structure, these clusters should correspond to known cell
types or states (for datasets with ground truth) or reveal interpretable substructure (for exploratory analysis).

Gene-level Clustering (Validation Baseline)
To assess whether transcript clustering adds value beyond standard approaches,
we independently clustered the same cells using gene counts:
• Data source: Official gene-count matrix from GEO for the Ding dataset (4k
cells × 28k genes).
• Processing: The standard COTAN clean() function applied to remove
low-quality features.
• Output: 15 clusters using identical clustering parameters as the transcript
analysis.
This baseline answers: does aggregating transcripts into genes lose information that affects cluster assignment? If both approaches yield similar results,
gene-level analysis suffices. If they diverge meaningfully, transcript data captures
something unique.
12

Comparison Approach
We compared the two clustering strategies through three lenses:
1. Confusion matrix: Cross-tabulate cluster assignments for all 4,077 common cells. High overlap suggests both methods capture similar structure;
systematic splitting/merging reveals where they diverge.
2. DTU re-run with swapped labels: Apply the DTU detection algorithm
to transcript-level data using gene-derived cluster assignments as ground
truth. This tests whether DTU signals are robust to how cells are partitioned,
or if they depend entirely on clustering resolution.
3. Event overlap analysis: Compare which genes/transcripts each approach
identifies as DTU-positive:
• Shared events: Robust signals surviving both clusterings.
• Transcript-exclusive: Subtle switches requiring fine-grained partitioning.
• Gene-exclusive: Weaker signals needing aggregated statistical power.
This multi-faceted comparison moves beyond asking which clustering is correct to a more nuanced question: what does each approach reveal, and when
should researchers prefer one over the other?

Rationale for the Three-Part Strategy
The validation design addresses three distinct concerns:
• Biological validity: Do clusters correspond to meaningful cell types (verified via marker genes and known biology)?
• Methodological contribution: Does transcript-level analysis add value
beyond standard gene-level approaches? If not, the extra complexity is unjustified.
• Robustness of DTU calls: Are detected isoform switches stable across
clustering strategies, or do they represent artifacts of a particular partitioning scheme?
By addressing all three concerns, we establish whether transcript-level COTAN
is not just feasible but genuinely useful for DTU detection.

3.5

DTU Detection Algorithm

The core contribution of this thesis is an algorithm that identifies Differential
Transcript Usage events from sparse single-cell data using COTAN’s statistical
framework. Each step has explicit biological and statistical justification.

Step-by-Step Procedure
For each gene with k ≥ 2 detected isoforms:
1. Mutual exclusivity screening: For every possible pair of isoforms (A, B)
within the gene:
• Compute co-expression score (coex) from 2×2 contingency tables (presence/absence across cells).
• Filter for negative coex with p-value < 0.05.
• Rationale: Mutual exclusivity indicates alternative splicing or isoform
switching.
2. Contrast calculation: For each significant pair, compute differential enrichment vectors:
∆cluster = DEAA (cluster) − DEAB (cluster),

(3.1)

where DEA is the COTAN differential expression score across all clusters.
Positive values indicate cluster-specific preference for isoform A; negative
values favor B .
3. Thresholding: Retain pairs showing bidirectional switching:
• At least one cluster with ∆ > τ .
• At least one cluster with ∆ < −τ .
• Thresholds: τ = 0.2 (human, well-defined cell lines), τ = 0.05 (mouse,
continuous differentiation).
Rationale: True DTU events should show opposing isoform preferences
across conditions, not just one-sided enrichment.
The algorithm outputs a list of gene-isoform pairs with evidence for clusterspecific differential usage, ranked by statistical significance and effect size (|∆|).
14

Biological Justification for Each Step
Why does this approach detect genuine DTU rather than technical artifacts? The
logic chain:
1. Mutual exclusivity (negative coex) rules out co-expression from shared regulation (e.g., both isoforms elevated in the same cell type).
2. Significant p-values control false discovery rates. With COTAN’s asymptotic chi-squared approximation across thousands of cells, these values are
well-calibrated.
3. Contrast thresholding (τ ) ensures biological relevance: we seek isoform
switches where A dominates in some conditions while B dominates in others—
not just random fluctuations around equal usage.
The key insight: by combining mutual exclusivity screening with bidirectional
contrast requirements, the algorithm filters for patterns consistent with genuine
alternative splicing rather than noise or technical bias.

3.6

Tuning the Threshold Parameter τ

The threshold parameter τ controls sensitivity versus specificity in DTU detection: high values yield few confident calls but risk missing subtle switches; low
values capture more events but increase false positives.
We developed a tuning script (requested by supervisor Prof. Silvia Galfré) that:
• Varies τ across a range (e.g., 0.01 to 0.5).
• Counts detected DTU events at each threshold.
• Plots the relationship between τ and the number of significant pairs.
• Suggests thresholds that yield biologically reasonable candidate counts (typically 20–100 events for validation feasibility).
The script is included in the GitHub repository alongside the main pipeline.
Results from this tuning informed our choice of τ = 0.2 for human data (wellseparated cell lines, stronger signals) and τ = 0.05 for mouse brain tissue (continuous differentiation trajectory, subtler transitions).
Detailed threshold optimization results appear in Chapter 4 alongside the DTU
discovery findings.

Core Finding: DTU Detection Results

(This section presents the main contribution of this thesis—the application of COTAN

Human Dataset Findings (Arrigoni)
The human cell line mixture provided ground-truth validation through known

The threshold parameter τ was tuned empirically to yield biologically reasonable

4.4

Discussion

We now contextualize these findings within broader methodological and biological frameworks.

Key Findings Interpretation
Three core conclusions emerge from the results:
1. Feasibility confirmed: COTAN’s contingency-table framework successfully detects DTU in sparse 3’-biased data. This extends its applicability
beyond gene-level co-expression to isoform-resolution analysis.
2. Biological plausibility validated: Top candidates (Meg3, Malat1, TPM1)
align with known biology—lncRNA switching in neurogenesis and ribosomal protein regulation. This suggests the algorithm recovers genuine signal
rather than technical artifacts.
3. Clustering matters: The divergence between transcript-level and genelevel DTU calls reveals that isoform switches can distinguish cellular substates invisible to aggregated counts. Fine-grained clustering unlocks detection of subtle transitions.
The convergence across datasets (human cell lines + mouse brain) strengthens
confidence: the method works under different biological contexts, not just a single
favorable case study.

Limitations and Caveats
Despite encouraging results, several constraints temper interpretation:
22

• 3’-bias invisibility: Alternative splicing events occurring in transcript midregions remain undetectable when only 3’ ends are sequenced. The DTU
candidates we find represent a subset—those where isoforms differ at their
polyadenylation sites or terminal exons.
• EM rounding effects: Fractional EM assignments were rounded to integers
for COTAN compatibility. Low-abundance isoforms with small fractional
counts may be distorted by this discretization, potentially introducing false
negatives or altered contrast values.
• Threshold sensitivity: The choice of τ lacks formal statistical grounding.
While empirical tuning produced reasonable candidate numbers, a permutationbased null distribution would provide calibrated p-values rather than arbitrary thresholds.
• Lack of orthogonal validation: Without long-read sequencing or targeted
RT-PCR confirmation, false positive rates remain estimated from internal
consistency (shared vs exclusive events) rather than ground truth. This is
a limitation common to computational DTU studies but remains critical for
biological claims.
These limitations suggest concrete directions for improvement: longer reads
for complete isoform coverage, better EM handling via fractional-count methods,
formal threshold calibration, and experimental validation of top candidates.

Future Directions: Extending COTAN for Transcript Analysis
The framework established here opens several avenues for methodological extension:
1. Integration with long-read data: Combining short-read depth (thousands
of cells) with PacBio/Nanopore isoform annotation would anchor DTU calls
in complete transcript sequences. This hybrid approach could validate computational predictions while scaling to population-level sample sizes.
2. Trajectory-aware analysis: Current clustering assumes discrete cell populations, but differentiation is continuous. Modeling isoform proportions
along pseudotime trajectories (e.g., using COTAN’s framework adapted for
regression rather than categorical comparison) could reveal dynamic switching patterns during transitions.
3. Allele-specific expression extension: COTAN’s contingency table approach naturally extends to phased variants. Detecting allele-biased isoform usage would connect regulatory genetics (eQTLs, sQTLs) to splicing
outcomes in single cells—bridging population genomics with transcript regulation.
23

4. Benchmarking against bulk DTU tools: Systematic comparison with
rMATS, SUPPA2, and DRIMSeq on matched pseudo-bulks would contextualize sensitivity and specificity. This benchmark could identify regimes
where single-cell methods add unique value versus when aggregation suffices.
These extensions remain beyond the scope of this thesis but demonstrate that
transcript-level COTAN is not a final solution—rather, it establishes proof-ofconcept for sparsity-robust isoform analysis in single cells. The framework’s flexibility suggests further adaptations as new data types and biological questions
emerge.

Chapter 5

Conclusions
This thesis established that COTAN’s sparsity-robust contingency-table framework can be extended to transcript-level analysis, recovering within-gene exclusivity patterns indicative of Differential Transcript Usage (DTU) from sparse
3′ -biased single-cell RNA sequencing data. The key insight—that zero patterns
carry reliable signal even in extremely sparse matrices—transfers successfully
from gene co-expression to isoform mutual exclusivity detection.

5.1

Summary of Contributions

Three primary contributions emerge from this work:
1. Methodological extension: COTAN’s statistical framework, originally designed for gene-gene co-expression analysis, was adapted for transcriptlevel DTU detection. The algorithm identifies mutually exclusive isoform
pairs through contingency-table independence tests and validates them via
bidirectional contrast thresholds across cell clusters.
2. Reproducible workflow: A complete pipeline for generating transcriptcount matrices from 3′ -biased scRNA-seq data was developed and documented. This includes modifications to alevin-fry pseudo-alignment (identity t2g mapping), custom Nextflow automation, and COTAN integration
scripts—all made available via the GitHub repository.
3. Empirical validation: Application to two datasets (human cell line mixture: 29k cells; mouse brain tissue: 4k cells) demonstrated feasibility. Top
candidates (Meg3, Malat1, TPM1) align with published biology despite the
challenging 3′ -bias constraint, validating that genuine isoform switches can
be recovered from droplet-based data.
The comparison between transcript-level and gene-level clustering revealed
a secondary but important finding: fine-grained cellular substructure captured
25

by isoform analysis enables detection of subtle DTU events invisible to aggregated counts. This suggests that as scRNA-seq datasets grow larger and deeper,
transcript-aware methods will become increasingly powerful for uncovering posttranscriptional regulation in single cells.

5.2

Broader Implications

This work has implications across three domains:
For computational genomics: The success of contingency-table statistics
at transcript resolution demonstrates that sparsity need not be treated as a problem to overcome (via imputation or log-transformation) but rather modeled directly. This philosophy may extend to other single-cell challenges—allele-specific
expression, chromatin accessibility peak co-occurrence, and multi-modal integration.
For isoform biology: Detecting DTU in 3′ -biased data opens new analysis opportunities for existing large-scale datasets (e.g., Human Cell Atlas, Tabula
Sapiens) that were previously unusable for isoform-level questions. While sensitivity is limited compared to full-length protocols, the trade-off between cell
numbers and coverage may favor droplet methods when statistical power from
thousands of cells outweighs per-cell depth.
For COTAN framework development: This thesis validates that COTAN’s
core design principles—modeling zeros as signal rather than noise, using large-m
χ2 approximations for p-value calibration—generalize beyond gene-level analysis.
Future extensions to other single-cell modalities (ATAC-seq peak co-occurrence,
protein abundance correlations in CITE-seq) appear feasible.

5.3

Limitations and Future Work

Despite encouraging results, several constraints must be acknowledged:
• 3′ -bias constraint: Alternative splicing events in transcript mid-regions
remain undetectable when only poly-A tails are sequenced. The DTU candidates we find represent those where isoforms differ at their 3′ ends (alternative polyadenylation, terminal exon choice).
• Lack of orthogonal validation: Without long-read sequencing or targeted
RT-PCR confirmation, false positive rates remain estimated from internal
consistency rather than ground truth. This is a critical next step for biological claims.
• Threshold sensitivity: The contrast threshold τ was tuned empirically.
Formal statistical calibration via permutation testing would improve reproducibility and enable cross-dataset comparisons.
26

• EM rounding artifacts: Fractional EM assignments were rounded to integers for COTAN compatibility, potentially distorting low-abundance isoforms.
Future work should address these limitations through:
1. Integration with long-read datasets (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore) for complete
isoform annotation.
2. Permutation-based threshold calibration and false discovery rate control.
3. Extension to trajectory-aware analysis along differentiation paths.
4. Benchmarking against bulk DTU tools on matched pseudo-bulks.

Final Remarks
This thesis demonstrates that transcript-level COTAN can reveal regulatory programs hidden to gene-centric analyses. The contingency-table framework provides a principled approach to sparse data analysis—one that respects the actual
measurement process rather than imposing transformations designed for dense,
normally distributed data.
The path forward is clear: with orthogonal validation and integration of complementary technologies (long-read sequencing, trajectory modeling), isoformaware single-cell methods will transition from proof-of-concept to standard practice. As datasets continue scaling toward millions of cells, the statistical power
from large sample sizes may compensate for per-cell sparsity, making DTU detection in droplet-based data increasingly viable.
The framework established here provides a foundation for that future work—
combining methodological rigor (contingency-table statistics), computational reproducibility (documented Nextflow pipeline), and biological validation (alignment with known isoform switches). Whether applied to neurogenesis, cancer
evolution, or developmental biology, the core question remains: which transcripts
are cells expressing, and how does that choice change across conditions? COTAN’s
answer—modeling zeros as signal rather than noise—offers a robust path forward
for sparse single-cell data analysis.
